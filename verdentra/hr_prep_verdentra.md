# HR Interview Prep — Verdentra
> Ready-to-rehearse answers tailored to your background.
> Say each answer out loud at least twice. Adjust any detail that doesn't feel natural.

---

## How to Use This

- These are **starting points**, not scripts. Make them sound like you.
- Keep answers between **60 and 90 seconds** when spoken — practice with a timer.
- Don't memorise word-for-word. Memorise the **key points** in each answer.
- After each answer, pause. Don't rush to fill silence.

---

## Q1 — "Tell me about yourself."

> *This is always the first question. It sets the tone. Keep it professional, forward-looking, and under 90 seconds.*

**Model Answer:**

"I'm a Full Stack Software Engineer with 4 years of experience building and shipping web applications across both frontend and backend stacks.

I started my career at MI Synergy where I got my foundation in backend development — Spring Boot APIs, database design, that kind of work. From there I moved to Velou, a US-based product company, where I built internal data tooling for major retail and fashion brands using Vue.js and Koa.js. That experience gave me a strong understanding of working with large, complex datasets and collaborating closely with product and data teams.

Most recently I was at Recurved Digital Solutions — an agency environment where I managed full-lifecycle development for multiple client brands simultaneously. I worked across React, Next.js, ASP.NET Core, Node.js, and cloud deployments on AWS. I also got the chance to mentor junior developers joining the team and integrate AI APIs into several client projects.

I'm now looking for a role where I can go deeper into the Microsoft stack — React and .NET specifically — and work on more complex, longer-term product challenges. Verdentra's focus on cloud and AI solutions for enterprise clients is exactly the kind of environment I want to grow in."

**Key points to remember:**
- MI Synergy → Velou → Recurved (always in order)
- End with why you're here — connect it to Verdentra

## Q2 — "Why are you leaving your current job?" / "Why did you leave Recurved?"

> *You're between jobs. Be honest, positive, and forward-looking. Never badmouth.*

**Model Answer:**

"Recurved was a great experience — I learned a lot about managing multiple client projects simultaneously and delivering under pressure. But agency environments, by nature, are about rapid delivery across many clients. I've reached a point in my career where I want to go deeper rather than wider — to work on a product or platform over a longer arc, see how architectural decisions play out over time, and grow with a team that's solving harder problems.

I made the deliberate decision to take some time between roles to sharpen my skills — specifically on the .NET side — and to be selective about the next opportunity rather than just taking the first thing available. Verdentra caught my attention because the work sits at the intersection of cloud, AI, and enterprise software delivery. That's exactly where I want to be."

**Key points:**
- Agency → product shift (positive framing)
- Deliberate decision to upskill during the gap
- Gap = intentional, not passive

## Q3 — "Where do you see yourself in 3–5 years?"

> *Show ambition but stay realistic. Don't say "in your role." Don't say "I don't know."*

**Model Answer:**

"In 3 to 5 years I see myself as a strong senior-level engineer who can lead technical decisions on a feature or service end-to-end — not just write good code, but contribute to architecture choices, mentor others, and collaborate closely with product and business stakeholders.

I'm particularly interested in deepening my understanding of distributed systems and cloud-native architectures — the kind of engineering challenges I'd get exposure to at Verdentra working with enterprise clients. I'd also like to take on more responsibility in terms of technical leadership over time — not necessarily management, but being the person the team looks to when there's a hard technical problem.

I think Verdentra is a great place to build toward that — the ISV partner model and the cloud and AI focus means I'd be working on genuinely interesting problems, not just maintaining legacy systems."

**Key points:**
- Senior engineer → technical leadership (not management necessarily)
- Distributed systems + cloud = realistic for this role
- Tie it back to what Verdentra offers

## Q4 — "What is your biggest weakness?"

> *Never say "I work too hard" or "I'm a perfectionist." Pick a real weakness that you're actively working on.*

**Model Answer:**

"Honestly, my biggest weakness has been in areas I haven't had much formal exposure to — specifically cloud infrastructure and DevOps. My production experience has been AWS-focused and mostly handled through existing setups. I haven't had as much hands-on experience configuring infrastructure from scratch or writing IaC.

I've been very deliberate about fixing that — I've been building a project that I'm deploying to Azure using Bicep for infrastructure as code and GitHub Actions for CI/CD. It's been a steep learning curve but I've made a lot of progress and I now have a much better end-to-end understanding of the deployment pipeline.

I think it's important to be honest about gaps rather than pretend they don't exist — and more importantly, to do something about them."

**Key points:**
- Real weakness (cloud/IaC)
- What you're actively doing about it (Task Tracker project)
- Ends with self-awareness, not self-pity

## Q6 — "What are your salary expectations?"

> *This is a negotiation. Don't anchor too low. Don't anchor too high without justification.*

**Model Answer (if asked directly):**

"Based on my 4 years of experience across full-stack development, cloud deployments, and the React and .NET stack, and considering market rates for this level of role in Sri Lanka, I'm looking for something in the range of **LKR [X] to [Y]** per month. That said, I'm also looking at the total package — growth opportunities, learning exposure, and the nature of the work matter to me as well. I'm open to a conversation about what the band looks like for this role."

**Guidance on the number:**
- Research Glassdoor / LinkedIn Salary / local developer communities for current Sri Lanka market rates for a 4-year full-stack engineer
- Start with a range where your target is the lower end — gives room to negotiate upward
- If they give you a number first, don't immediately accept — say "let me think about that" and counter with your range

**If they ask "is that negotiable?":**
> "I'm open to discussion — the priority for me is the right role and team fit. The compensation should reflect that."

**If they give a number below your range:**
> "I appreciate the offer. Based on my experience with the React and .NET stack and my background in cloud deployments, I was hoping for something closer to [your number]. Is there flexibility there?"

## Q7 — "Describe a conflict with a teammate and how you handled it."

> *Pick a real situation. Focus on the resolution, not the drama.*

**Model Answer:**

"At Recurved, there was a situation where a colleague and I disagreed about how to approach authentication for a new client project. They wanted to use a third-party auth service to move faster; I thought we should implement JWT-based auth ourselves because the client had specific requirements around token structure that the third-party solution couldn't accommodate cleanly.

Rather than escalating it or just pushing my position, I suggested we each write up the trade-offs quickly — time to implement, flexibility, maintenance cost — and review them together. When we laid it out objectively, it was clear that the custom approach was actually the right call for this specific project's requirements, and my colleague agreed once they could see the constraints clearly.

The project shipped on time, the auth worked exactly as the client needed, and I think we both came out of it with a better habit of documenting decisions before debating them. That's something I've carried into every project since."

**Key points:**
- Real technical disagreement (not interpersonal drama)
- You proposed a structured resolution (trade-off writeup)
- Outcome was positive for the project AND the relationship

## Q8 — "Tell me about a time you failed and what you learned."

> *Be honest. The failure should be real. The learning should be specific and genuine.*

**Model Answer:**

"Early in my time at Velou, I was working on a UI feature that involved handling a large dataset — thousands of records rendered in a filterable list. I shipped it without proper performance testing and it worked fine in development, but in production with real data volumes, the rendering was noticeably slow. Users started complaining about lag.

I had to go back, profile the component, identify that the list was re-rendering unnecessarily on every filter change, and implement proper memoisation. It was fixed quickly but I'd already caused a poor user experience for a day.

What I learned was that 'it works on my machine' is not a definition of done. From that point on, I started testing with realistic data volumes before calling anything complete, and I got much more careful about profiling component performance before shipping UI-heavy features. It also taught me to be more upfront with the team when I notice potential performance risks early — rather than hoping it won't matter."

**Key points:**
- Real failure (performance bug in production)
- Specific fix (memoisation, profiling)
- Concrete behaviour change — not vague "I learned to be more careful"

## Q9 — "How do you handle tight deadlines and pressure?"

> *Show that you have a system, not just willpower.*

**Model Answer:**

"I've worked in agency environments where tight deadlines are the norm, so I've had to develop a system rather than just relying on working harder.

When a deadline is tight, the first thing I do is break down the work into what's genuinely required for the scope versus what's nice-to-have. A lot of pressure comes from trying to do everything at once — scoping clearly up front removes a lot of that.

I also communicate early when I can see a risk. I'd rather tell a project manager on day one that a feature is likely to take two days longer than I thought, than tell them the night before delivery. Early warnings give the team options; last-minute warnings just create panic.

In terms of the actual execution — I work in focused blocks, I prioritise blocking issues first, and I don't hesitate to ask for help or clarification when something is ambiguous rather than spending hours going in the wrong direction.

At Recurved, we regularly had to deliver across multiple client timelines simultaneously. I got comfortable with the discomfort of pressure — it's part of the job. The key is staying calm enough to make good decisions under it."

**Key points:**
- System: scope triage, early communication, focused blocks
- Real context: Recurved multi-client environment
- Calm under pressure — not "I just push harder"

## Q10 — "What motivates you?"

> *Be genuine. Avoid generic answers like "I love solving problems."*

**Model Answer:**

"A few things genuinely motivate me.

The first is the feeling of shipping something and knowing it works — not just technically, but for the person using it. There's a satisfaction in building something that solves a real problem cleanly, especially when the implementation is elegant.

The second is learning. I get a lot of energy from understanding something I didn't understand before — whether that's a new pattern, a new tool, or just a better way to think about a problem I thought I already understood. That's part of why I've been deliberately working on the .NET and Azure stack recently — I find the learning process genuinely engaging, not just a box to tick.

And the third — which I think is underrated — is working with people who care about doing things properly. Not perfectly, but properly. When you're around teammates who take the craft seriously, the standard of your own work goes up naturally. That environment is something I look for in a team."

**Key points:**
- Shipping working software (tangible outcome)
- Learning (genuine, not performative — backed by your recent upskilling)
- Team quality (shows you care about culture, not just salary)

## Final Tips for the Day

**Before you go in:**
- Get a good sleep. Tired brain = slow answers.
- Review your FlowForge talking point and "tell me about yourself" one more time
- Have a question ready to ask them (see below)

**Questions to ask Verdentra (pick 1–2):**
- *"What does the first 90 days typically look like for someone in this role — how do you onboard new engineers?"*
- *"What's the balance between greenfield work and maintaining existing systems?"*
- *"How are engineers involved in technical decision-making — is it top-down or more collaborative?"*
- *"What does growth look like here for a developer 2–3 years in?"*

**Asking good questions shows:**
- You're evaluating them too (not desperate)
- You're thinking about how you'll succeed there
- You're interested in more than just getting hired

---

> [!TIP]
> The goal of an HR interview is not to prove you're perfect. It's to show you're self-aware, honest, and someone they'd enjoy working with. Confidence without arrogance. Honesty without oversharing. Calm without being robotic.
